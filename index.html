Product designer

The messier the problem, the more interesting it gets.

Give me a tangled workflow, a complicated data model or a product nobody quite knows how to build yet. That’s where I feel at home.

01 · Philosophy

I’d rather make the problem smaller than the interface smarter.

When faced with a complicated workflow, my first instinct is to understand why it’s complicated in the first place.

At Arrival, that meant automating the allocation of software across vehicle controllers rather than making the allocation process easier to navigate.

At Etoso, it meant questioning how much of a planned feature actually required human involvement. More than half of it turned out to be something we could automate altogether.

What excites me most about design is the opportunity to change how things work, not just how people interact with them.

02 · Impact

Selected impact

From sequential to parallel.

Arrival

Helped transform a traditionally sequential automotive software development process into parallel workflows, allowing different engineering teams to work more independently. The solution was eventually adopted in the production process for Arrival’s van prototypes.

Less to build. Five times faster to use.

Etoso

Rethought an internal data verification tool, automating more than half of its planned functionality and assembling most of the rest from existing product modules. Significantly less development effort, five times faster work for the experts.

One workflow. Not another collection of tools.

SODA.Auto

Challenged the separation of requirements and test management into individual applications, bringing them together into a continuous engineering experience where requirements, architecture, tests and validation could be connected.

03 · How my brain works

How I approach product design

  1. Understand before designing.

    At Arrival, I spent a lot of time bothering hardware and software engineers with questions. I wanted to understand the technical reasoning behind their requirements, not just what they needed from an interface. Getting to know an unfamiliar domain is one of my favourite parts of starting a new project.

  2. Question the brief.

    I treat requirements as the beginning of a conversation. At Etoso, questioning the original scope of an internal data verification tool helped me discover that most of the planned functionality didn’t actually need human involvement. A little extra thinking can sometimes save weeks of design and development.

  3. Think in systems.

    I love working on use cases and data models simultaneously. Understanding how entities relate to each other helps me shape the workflow itself, identify who’s involved at each stage, what exactly they’re working with, what information they need and how their work connects to everyone else’s.

  4. Design what’s worth building.

    Years of working closely with engineers have given me a pretty good intuition for what’s straightforward to implement and what might turn into a surprisingly expensive undertaking. It helps me explore alternatives with engineering and find pragmatic solutions without compromising what makes the product valuable to its users.

  5. Start small. Think ahead.

    I like to explore what a product could eventually become before deciding what its first version should be. Then I look for the smallest meaningful version we can ship, making sure today’s design decisions leave enough room for the product to evolve without having to rethink everything from scratch.

  6. Make ideas buildable.

    I genuinely enjoy thinking through edge cases, documenting behaviour, defining states and annotating interactions. There’s something particularly satisfying about handing over a complicated feature and seeing engineers build it without a hundred follow-up questions. Knowing that they enjoy working with my designs makes it even more rewarding.

04 · It’s a team sport

Working with teams

My favourite kind of disagreement.

At SODA.Auto, I worked closely with a technical lead who knew the engineering domain inside out. We disagreed quite a lot at first. Eventually, his deep understanding of engineering processes and my focus on the user experience became a really powerful combination.

SODA.Auto — Working with engineering

It started with conversations in the kitchen.

At Arrival, informal chats with designers from neighbouring teams grew into cross-team design reviews. I started helping them advocate for their work, and eventually became an informal design lead across the department, helping teams hire and mentor designers.

Arrival — Design leadership

When ideas start coming to you.

At Etoso, I started picking up little ideas from everyday conversations with engineers and analysts, finding opportunities to bring them into the product. Over time, people started actively coming to me with their ideas. I loved seeing how that changed their relationship with design.

Etoso — Building trust

From the last conversation to the first.

When I joined Etoso, AI features were largely defined by engineers before design got involved. Over time, as we built trust and learned to work together, design became part of the conversation from the very beginning.

Etoso — Cross-functional collaboration

The hipsters.

At Arrival, our technical director used to jokingly call us hipsters while we were developing our toolchain. After we demonstrated the proof of concept, he came to our room specifically to tell us how impressed he was with what we’d built.

Still one of my favourite moments.
